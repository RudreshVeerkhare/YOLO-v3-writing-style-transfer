What's This About

Nature and engineered systems love messy geometry: tangled airways, porous rocks, burrs on a leaf — you name it. Those shapes matter. They determine heat flow, diffusion, and all the PDE-driven physics we care about. [Figure: fig:teaser]

We have good tools for pictures: Monte Carlo ray tracing practically changed rendering (see Kajiya 1986, Veach 1997) [Kajiya:86][Veach:97]. But simulation — solving PDEs on wildly complex domains — still often resorts to brutal simplifications: coarsening, homogenization, or learned surrogates. Those tricks work, but they throw away small-scale structure that can be essential. Look, this doesn’t work great on small objects. We tried. Small-scale geometry often controls the macro behavior, and naive smoothing or a bad mesh can bias the solution badly.

The naive thing and why it stinks

The usual playbook is: build a mesh, discretize, solve. Or pick a finite basis, project your PDE and go. That works for many domains, but meshes fail spectacularly on complex geometry (tunnels, thin sheets, tiny cavities), and basis methods introduce aliasing or require huge bases to capture fine detail. The Walk on Spheres (WoS) family of methods takes a different tack: instead of discretizing the domain, they march random walks that only need to know the closest boundary point — like ray tracing but for PDEs (classic WoS: Müller 1956) [Muller:56].

Prompted by the gap between rendering and simulation, Sawhney et al. argued for pushing grid-free Monte Carlo methods as a general way to solve PDEs on extremely complicated domains [Sawhney:2020:MCGP]. The key idea is simple and attractive: you don't need a mesh or a global basis, you just need pointwise access to geometry (closest-point queries, distance oracles, etc.). That sidesteps aliasing and most mesh headaches.

But there's a catch

Almost all practical grid-free work so far sticks to Dirichlet boundary conditions — you know, "value at the boundary is fixed." Neumann or mixed Neumann–Dirichlet conditions (where the normal derivative is specified on parts of the boundary) are a different beast: they require handling normal derivatives, reflections, or local boundary solves, and naive extensions of WoS either blow up the variance or become biased. In short: grid-free is promising, but Neumann BCs have been the hang-up. That's the problem we aim to fix.

What Others Have Tried

Short orientation: there are two broad families of PDE solvers people reach for. One punches the domain into pieces (FEM, BEM, meshless FEM) and approximates functions on those pieces; the other—grid‑free Monte Carlo—lets Brownian motion do the heavy lifting and never builds a mesh. If you want a careful comparison of the tradeoffs, see Sawhney et al. for a focused treatment and numerical comparisons [Sawhney:2020:MCGP, Sawhney:2022:VCWoS].

Walk on Spheres: old trick, new interest

The Walk on Spheres (WoS) estimator goes way back to Müller (1956) and recently got a second life after being promoted for rendering and other geometry‑heavy tasks [Muller:1956:WOS, Sawhney:2020:MCGP]. The core idea is wonderfully simple: simulate where Brownian motion first hits the boundary by taking giant spherical hops until you land near the boundary, then read off the boundary condition. No mesh, no basis functions, and therefore no aliasing from basis truncation.

Since that revival, work has split into two practical directions:

  • Speedups and implementation tricks. People have squeezed a lot more efficiency out of WoS: careful low‑level implementations and data structures [Mossberg:2021:GAM], bidirectional formulations that trade math for fewer samples [Qi:2022:BidirWOS], and sample caching/reuse strategies to amortize cost across queries [Miller:2023:BVC]. These are engineering + probability wins: the estimator stays unbiased but you get many fewer expensive geometry queries.
  • Extending the geometry and boundary repertoire. There's steady progress making WoS work on more complex shapes and faster boundary‑proximity tests. But here’s the blunt truth: practical grid‑free WoS methods have been largely focused on Dirichlet boundaries. Handling Neumann (or mixed) boundary conditions is not a trivial add‑on. The naive idea—mirror the Brownian paths at the boundary (reflecting Brownian motion)—is conceptually natural but painful in practice without discretizing the boundary or introducing bias. Sawhney et al. discuss these limits and why they matter in applications [Sawhney:2022:VCWoS].

The fine print (what people often miss)

There are a couple of popular misconceptions worth calling out. First: "WoS means no geometry work" — not true. You still need robust nearest‑point and distance queries, and those become the practical bottleneck on complicated CAD models. Second: "Monte Carlo = always worse than FEM for accuracy" — also too simplistic. Monte Carlo avoids bias from basis truncation and meshes, and for some tasks (lots of independent point evaluations, very irregular domains) it can be superior in practice [Sawhney:2020:MCGP].

Bottom line: WoS and its modern variants are powerful, especially when you want to dodge meshing and global bases. But if your problem needs Neumann or mixed boundary conditions, or if fine boundary detail matters, the existing grid‑free toolset has important gaps—exactly the gap our work aims to address.

Non-Visible Regions

Since Brownian motion can effectively "walk around corners" (BMvsRBM), the BIE has no visibility term. Hence, the solution value at $x_k$ can depend on non-visible points \(x_k+1\), complicating use of directional sampling. In particular, if the subdomain around \(x_k\) is nonconvex, then a na\"ive strategy is to estimate \(u_x+1\) at all intersections along a ray from \(x_k\) (MultipleIntersectionsVsStarSampling, left), yielding a branching walk that increases in size exponentially. One could instead use just a single randomly selected intersection, but this approach yields extremely high variance (see MultipleIntersections), for two reasons. First, the recursive solution estimate must be multiplied by the number of intersections to account for the expected contribution from each intersection, causing a blowup in value as walk length increases. Second, the Poisson kernel alternates sign along consecutive intersection points along a ray, yielding unstable estimates due to cancellation kalos2009monte.

(One might overcome these issues by decomposing the BIE into independent integrals—each with a Poisson kernel that is entirely positive or negative—then randomly select one integral for sampling in proportion to the area over which it is integrated. Unfortunately, it is unclear how to efficiently perform such a decomposition.)

What We Actually Need From the Geometry

Walk-on-Stars (WoSt) is gloriously grid-free, but it still needs a few paid-for services from whatever boundary representation (B-rep) you hand it. Nothing exotic — just four query primitives. If your B-rep can answer these, WoSt can run on it:

  • Closest-point queries to \(\partial \mathcal{D}_D\): given a walk location \(x\), where is the nearest Dirichlet boundary point?
  • Closest-silhouette-point queries to \(\partial \mathcal{D}_N\): from \(x\) find the nearest point on the Neumann boundary that can be seen as a silhouette in the direction of interest (more below).
  • Ray-intersection queries against \(\partial \mathcal{D}_N\): cast rays for visibility and intersection tests on Neumann patches.
  • Point-sampling queries on \(\partial \mathcal{D}_N\): draw boundary points according to some importance sampling scheme for the Neumann contributions.

Important practical note: none of these queries require the mesh to have a well-defined inside/outside. Your B-rep can be messy — non-watertight, cracked, have holes or even self-intersections — and WoSt will still ask for the same four things. We do, however, assume the B-rep is accurate enough for closest-point and ray tests; extreme geometric noise or massively inconsistent normals will make these queries noisy and hurt convergence. For how we handle open or double-sided domains, see WalkOnStarsDoubleSided (discussion in the codebase/paper).

Short version: give us these four queries, and we avoid meshing headaches. But don’t expect miracles from garbage geometry — WoSt is forgiving, not magical.

Finding the Silhouette — The Naive Way Fails Fast

Silhouette edges are where a triangle mesh switches from front-facing to back-facing relative to some view direction \(v\). For a given edge \(e\) shared by two triangles with consistently oriented normals \(n_1\) and \(n_2\), the local silhouette condition is:

\[\label{eq:SilhouetteDefinition} (v \cdot n_1) \cdot (v \cdot n_2) \leq 0, \]

Intuition: one triangle is facing you and the other is facing away, so the edge is a potential silhouette. Note that every true mesh boundary edge also satisfies this, so boundary edges are a special case of silhouette edges.

A naive implementation: ask a BVH for the closest point to \(x\) on every edge, then test each candidate to see if it's a silhouette. Sounds fine until you realize how many edges are geometrically nearby but not actually silhouettes for the direction you care about. The BVH will return lots of false positives, and you'll spend most of your time rejecting candidates. In short: correct, but painfully inefficient.

Why it matters for WoSt: when walking we repeatedly need the nearest silhouette point from the current walk location. Doing that with brute-force closest-edge queries kills performance on complex meshes. So we need geometry-aware pruning that understands orientation, not just position.

Teach the BVH About Normals (SNCH — the short name)

If the naive silhouette search fails because the BVH doesn't know about triangle orientation, the obvious fix is to make the BVH care about normals. We do that by augmenting the BVH with a Spatialized Normal Cone Hierarchy (SNCH) — yes, this idea comes from prior work in geometric processing [Johnson 2001]. Each SNCH node stores:

  • an axis-aligned bounding box (AABB), and
  • a normal cone: an axis vector (the average normal of all triangles in the node) and a half-angle \(\phi\) equal to the maximum angular deviation of any triangle normal in that node.

Geometric intuition: the normal cone is a compact, conservative summary of the orientations inside the node. If the query direction is far outside that cone, the whole node is either front-facing or back-facing and can be skipped for silhouette tests.

This is not new — Johnson et al. introduced the concept and detailed its construction — but it's exactly the tool we need to make silhouette queries practical on large meshes (see Johnson:2001:SNCH) [source: Johnson et al., 2001].

Limitations and honesty: normal cones are conservative summaries. If a node contains a wide spread of normals, its cone half-angle \(\phi\) will be large and the pruning power drops. In practice, good mesh partitioning keeps cones tight and yields huge savings; with pathological meshes you get less benefit.

Traverse the SNCH — Prune, Then Inspect

We perform silhouette queries by a depth-first traversal of the SNCH rooted at the BVH root. The trick: at each node we build a view cone centered at the query point \(x\).

Construction: the view-cone axis points to the node's AABB center and its half-angle tightly bounds the AABB as seen from \(x\). Now test whether the view cone and the node's normal cone can jointly contain a pair of mutually orthogonal directions. If they cannot, then every triangle in the node is either front-facing or back-facing from \(x\), so the node cannot contain a silhouette edge and can be pruned.

Why this is elegant: it's a cheap, conservative test that combines position and orientation info. If the test succeeds we descend; if it fails we skip potentially large chunks of geometry. This is much faster than asking for closest edges and checking silhouettes one by one.

Honesty about edge cases: the test is conservative, not exact. Because cones approximate both position (via the AABB bound) and orientation (via the normal cone), borderline situations (very thin features, extremely close triangles with wildly differing normals) can force descent even if no silhouette exists. Conversely, a badly constructed SNCH with overly large cones can reduce pruning power. Empirically this strategy works well on typical meshes, but expect degraded performance on adversarial geometry.

For nonzero Neumann boundary conditions we must sample boundary points on \(\partial \mathcal{D}_N\) to evaluate the Neumann integrals. Random uniform sampling is simple but inefficient; most samples contribute almost nothing if they are far or occluded. So we borrow a trick from rendering: hierarchical importance sampling to bias samples toward regions that matter [Estevez 2018].

Practical recipe we use:

  1. Traverse the BVH top-down. At each internal node, randomly select one child (not both) whose AABB intersects the spherical sampling domain \(\mathcal{S}\!\mathcal{S}\!\mathcal{D}(x)\) we are interested in.
  2. Bias that random choice to favor children that are "more relevant" to the current walk location \(x\) (closer or with larger visible area). The bias is a lightweight heuristic applied during traversal so we still get sublinear selection time.
  3. Stop when you reach a leaf and sample a point on that triangle according to its local area or a local importance function.

This single-child stochastic descent is cheap and focuses samples into promising parts of the boundary; it's basically next-event estimation from the graphics world [source: Estevez et al., 2018]. It’s not optimal importance sampling in a theoretical sense, but it gives a practical win: fewer wasted samples and better contribution estimates for the Neumann term.

Caveats: the method relies on having a sensible definition of the sampling domain \(\mathcal{S}\!\mathcal{S}\!\mathcal{D}(x)\) and a reasonable heuristic for preferring children. If the heuristic is poor, the algorithm can bias samples badly; if the domain test is too conservative you lose samples that should have been considered. Still — in our experiments on typical geometry, hierarchical importance sampling reduces variance and runtime compared to blind uniform sampling.

Reference for the hierarchical sampling idea: Estevez et al., 2018 on importance sampling in rendering (used here as an algorithmic template) [source: Estevez 2018].

Practical Evaluation (Yes, We Benchmarked It)

We won't pretend this is purely theoretical — here are the nitty-gritty details you actually care about when running WoSt in the wild. Implementation is multicore CPU-based and, in our experience, scales essentially linearly with cores on a shared-memory machine. Unless noted otherwise, the experiments reported below ran on a 64-core 3rd‑generation Intel Xeon workstation.

Preprocessing is cheap relative to the sampling work. Building a bounding-volume hierarchy (BVH) to accelerate distance queries and nearest-boundary finds typically consumes only a small fraction of total runtime for the problems we tried; this matches prior observations in Monte Carlo geometry work [Sawhney:2020:MCGP]. In short: don’t panic about BVH cost — it’s worth the payoff for faster per-walk queries.

Below we describe stopping tolerances (how we decide a walk is "close enough" to the boundary), how the estimator converges, where the method beats alternatives, and where it struggles.

Stopping Tolerances — Where We Trade Bias for Speed

Like classic Walk on Spheres (WoS), WoSt terminates walks when they enter an $\varepsilon$-shell around the Dirichlet boundary $\partial\mathcal{D}_D^{\varepsilon}$. That gives you a controllable, small bias: the smaller $\varepsilon$, the closer the terminal point is to the true boundary value, but the more steps you pay for. This is the usual bias–cost tradeoff; see Binder et al. for rate analyses in related WoS settings [Binder:2012:WosRate].

There’s a second knob: a minimum star radius $r^{\min}$ that prevents WoSt from trying to fit arbitrarily small star-shaped steps, which is important near tight concavities on Neumann parts of the boundary. Practically, $r^{\min}$ can introduce bias where the true geometry forces very small steps; compared to $\varepsilon$, $r^{\min}$ is often the more sensitive parameter for bias vs speed.

Empirically, we keep things simple: scale models to fit in a unit sphere and set $\varepsilon = r^{\min} = 10^{-3}$ unless otherwise stated. That’s not a magic number — it’s a pragmatic compromise that gives low bias for many shapes while keeping walk lengths manageable. You can do better: adapting $r^{\min}$ from local geometry (for example, based on local curvature or distance-to-concavity) usually helps, but picking a robust adaptive rule is problem-dependent and left as a practical tuning option rather than a requirement.

Short, honest summary: small $\varepsilon$ and small $r^{\min}$ reduce bias, but they cost you in walk steps. If you have lots of tiny geometric features and you want high accuracy there, expect longer runs — we tried, and it shows up in runtime.

Convergence — What the Error Looks Like

Call it Monte Carlo reality: variance falls like $O(1/N)$ while the standard error falls like $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ with the number of independent walks $N$. In other words, doubling the standard error requires roughly four times as many walks. Any bias introduced by $\varepsilon$ or $r^{\min}$ tends to be small compared to sampling noise for the $N$ we use, so in practice variance (not bias) usually dominates overall error.

We observe predictable structure in where variance shows up. Regions dominated by Neumann boundary conditions typically have higher variance — walks bounce around longer, the path integrals accumulate more randomness, and so you need more samples to get the same precision as in Dirichlet-dominated regions.

By the way, alternative SDE-based schemes behave differently: many project a path that leaves the domain back onto the boundary and continue the stochastic dynamics there. That changes walk geometry and makes variance/bias tradeoffs and step-size selection more subtle (see related SDE literature).

How We Stack Up Against Other Monte Carlo Tricks

Short answer: WoSt is typically both faster and less biased than earlier Monte Carlo approaches for mixed Dirichlet–Neumann problems when you pick comparable parameter settings. We compared representative alternatives using reasonable, comparable parameters: $r^{\min}=10^{-3}$ for WoSt, a reflection offset $\zeta=10^{-2}$ for reflected-WoS variants, and step size $\ell=10^{-4}$ for a representative SDE-based method. Those settings are chosen to give each method decent accuracy without absurd runtimes.

Why does WoSt tend to win?

  1. WoS with ad-hoc reflections accumulates bias: if you reflect long walks back into the domain repeatedly, small reflection offsets can systematically nudge the effective boundary condition and that bias compounds with walk length.
  2. SDE-based integrators need tiny steps to control bias near boundaries, and that kills runtime unless you accept more bias.
  3. WoSt trades a modest, controlled geometric restriction (the star step with $r^{\min}$) for much shorter walks and fewer boundary interactions, which reduces both runtime and accumulated bias in many cases.

That said, there’s no free lunch: in geometries with extremely tight, irregular concavities, WoSt’s $r^{\min}$ creates the familiar tension — you can reduce $r^{\min}$ and pay in runtime, or tolerate a bit more bias. We’re honest about that; the method makes the tradeoff explicit and tunable rather than hiding it in a tiny integrator step size.

Pure Neumann Problems — The Constant Problem (Handled, but Be Careful)

Classic fact: Poisson with pure Neumann boundary conditions is determined only up to an additive constant. WoSt behaves sensibly here: the early stages of random walks capture high‑frequency, local solution structure, while the contribution from the very long tail of a walk tends to be close to a constant shift across the domain. We see this in practice — high-frequency detail appears quickly, the slow modes take many samples to pin down.

To make the problem well-posed numerically, we use Tikhonov regularization to fix the additive constant (this is standard practice and gives a stable estimator). Tikhonov regularization does introduce a small bias in the global mean, but it stabilizes the inverse problem so variance doesn't blow up — a pragmatic tradeoff that most practitioners accept.

Honest limits: if you need the exact global mean to machine precision for a pure-Neumann problem, Monte Carlo methods will insist on a lot of samples (or auxiliary constraints). But if you care about local features, WoSt gets those right quickly.

Geometry Stress Tests — We Threw Weird Shapes at It

One of our motivations was to move Monte Carlo PDE solvers closer to the same kind of geometric mess encountered in graphics and natural scenes. So we built three synthetic stress tests that emphasize (i) ridiculously complex geometry and (ii) large Neumann boundary regions. These are not physics-accurate simulations; they are diagnostics for solver robustness and scaling.

To anchor intuition: thermal problems are a good analogy. Thermal radiation is a first-order transport problem well-served by classical Monte Carlo light transport techniques, while conduction (and much of convection modeling) is diffusion-like and needs a second-order treatment such as WoS/WoSt [Bati:2023:CCC]. Turbulent convective flow or full Navier–Stokes coupling can be handled with different stochastic techniques in the literature (e.g., some WoS-based fluid Monte Carlo work [Rioux-Lavoie:2022:MCFluid]), but that’s beyond our present scope.

Takeaway: WoSt is flexible enough to handle very complicated geometry without meshing, and the BVH + star-step strategy keeps runtimes reasonable. The limitations show up in the usual places — very small geometric features and extreme concavities — where you must either accept slower runs or tune $\varepsilon$ and $r^{\min}$ more aggressively.

The Fine Print and What Comes Next

Let's be candid: the main win of WoSt isn't a magic cure for Neumann boundary conditions. The real victory is that WoSt restores a WoS-like speed vs. bias tradeoff for these problems — big jumps and low-variance estimates when you're away from Dirichlet parts of the boundary. In practice that means much fewer tiny steps and less variance than naive SDE discretizations or some walk-on-ball (WoB) variants, especially on the open parts of the domain. Classic walk-on-spheres intuition still applies (see Müller 1956) [Müller, 1956] and standard Monte Carlo boundary discussions (see Sabelfeld 1991) [Sabelfeld, 1991].

But "works better" is not the same as "perfect." Neumann and mixed problems are intrinsically stickier than pure Dirichlet ones, and here's why.

Why Neumann / mixed problems are harder (short version)

  • Local-time / flux estimation is noisy: Neumann conditions force you to estimate boundary fluxes or local time accumulated at the boundary. That quantity concentrates on small boundary patches and is high-variance unless you sample them carefully.
  • Small features and thin channels kill step size: WoSt's big-step advantage evaporates when the geometry has small bumps, thin passages, or very close Dirichlet/Neumann interfaces — the algorithm must take small spheres, so cost spikes. Look, this doesn't work great on very small objects. We tried.
  • Mixed boundaries create conflicting behavior: Dirichlet parts want you to stop; Neumann parts want you to gather flux information and continue. Switching between those modes without bias is delicate.
  • Boundary geometry matters in non-obvious ways: local curvature, corners, and non-smooth parts change the hitting distribution. Naive radial sampling or local linearization can introduce bias unless handled carefully.

Exact Robin-type interface (what we'd like to support directly)

Many practical problems are not pure Neumann/Dirichlet but Robin-style. We explicitly want to account for boundary conditions of the form

$$\label{eq:RobinBoundaryConditions}\alpha u(x) + \beta \frac{\partial u(x)}{\partial n_x} = g(x)\ \text{ on } \partial \domain, \quad \alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{R}$$

Handling arbitrary $(\alpha,\beta)$ robustly requires accurate local models of both the solution and its normal derivative at the hit point. That is doable but increases per-hit work and can re-introduce bias if the local geometry/model is wrong.

Practical limitations (what actually bites us)

  • Variance near Neumann parts: even with large steps elsewhere, estimates of flux are high-variance unless you use variance-reduction tricks.
  • Computational hotspots: tiny geometric features force many small steps — algorithmic cost can blow up locally.
  • Implementation complexity: exact handling of corners, cusps, or non-smooth boundary data needs careful local solvers or hybridization with deterministic methods.
  • Not black-box: "mesh-free" ≠ "parameter-free". Step-size rules, stopping criteria, and local estimators need tuning for hard geometries.

Where to go from here — concrete research directions

Here are realistic, high-payoff extensions (and why):

  1. Adaptive local radii and geometry-aware stopping. Instead of uniform sphere rules, adapt the sampling radius to local feature size and curvature. This keeps the big-step advantage where geometry allows it and avoids over-sampling in tight regions.
  2. Robust Robin handling. Build or learn a local solver that outputs both $u$ and $\partial_n u$ at hit points so we can support arbitrary $$\alpha u + \beta \partial_n u = g$$ without hand-tuned approximations.
  3. Variance reduction: control variates, stratification, antithetic sampling, and quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC). Control variates that use cheap local PDE solves can knock down variance a lot.
  4. Hybrid methods: couple WoSt with local mesh or BEM solves near small features or corners. Let WoSt roam where it's efficient; hand over to a deterministic solver when geometry forces tiny steps.
  5. Path-space techniques: explore next-event, path-space, and bidirectional estimators (see below). These can dramatically reduce variance for rare or boundary-dominated contributions, but they need careful design for mixed boundaries.
  6. Theoretical work: sharp bounds on bias/variance as a function of geometry and stopping tolerance, and complexity estimates (cost vs. target error) are still open and important.
  7. Engineering: GPU/parallel implementations, efficient boundary queries (BVHs/k-d trees), and robust signed-distance computations make the method practical on complex CAD-like domains.
  8. Extensions: time-dependent (heat) or anisotropic diffusion and nonlinear problems via local linearization or iterative Monte Carlo solves.

Next-Event, Path-Space, and Bidirectional Estimators

Short, honest take: these estimators are promising but not plug-and-play. The naive WoSt estimate samples hitting points and accumulates contributions; next-event and path-space ideas try to reduce variance by targeting important events (e.g., the first hit to a small Neumann patch) or by combining forward and backward samplers to concentrate effort where it matters.

Why they might help: if most of the variance comes from a tiny set of boundary regions, explicitly sampling or conditioning on those events reduces wasted samples. Bidirectional schemes that grow samples from both the interior and the boundary and then couple them can reduce the effective path length and concentrate sampling power.

Why they're tricky: implementing them without introducing bias is subtle. Conditioning changes the path distribution and requires exact reweighting; coupling forward/backward paths in complex geometry is algorithmically heavy. We're not entirely sure which estimator is best in every regime — empirical study and careful variance analysis are needed.

Final note (real talk)

WoSt moves the needle: it brings the big-step, low-variance advantages of WoS closer to problems with Neumann and mixed boundaries. But it doesn't eliminate the fundamental difficulties — flux estimation, small geometric features, and mixed behavior still demand clever sampling, variance reduction, or hybridization. We're optimistic: the path forward is a mix of smarter local models, hybrid deterministic–stochastic coupling, and modern variance-reduction tactics. If you want to push this into production for messy geometries, expect to combine methods rather than rely on a single silver bullet.

Open Domains: Both Sides of the Wall (Double‑Sided Boundaries)

Short version: when your domain is open (think a surface floating in space) the solution can be different on the two sides of that surface. You can't pretend the boundary is single‑sided or you'll miss the jump terms. The correct way to handle this in a grid‑free WalkOnStars framework is to use the double‑sided boundary integral equation and treat the boundary contributions as Monte‑Carlo estimands. This is fiddlier and higher‑variance than the usual WoS setup, but it keeps the domain mesh‑free and exact in the sense of Green's identities.

What the BIE looks like (the ugly but necessary truth)

We start from the boundary integral equation for double‑sided traces (see Costabel 1987). For a point x (either on the boundary or in the interior) the representation reads

$$ \alpha(x)\,u(x) = \int_{\partial\Omega}\! P^+(x,z)\bigl[u^+(z)-u^-(z)\bigr]\;\mathrm{d}z - \int_{\partial\Omega}\! G(x,z)\Bigl[\frac{\partial u^+(z)}{\partial n^+_z} - \frac{\partial u^-(z)}{\partial n^-_z}\Bigr]\;\mathrm{d}z + \int_{\Omega} G(x,y)\,f(y)\;\mathrm{d}y. $$

Here:

  • $n^+$ and $n^-$ are the outward and inward unit normals on $\partial\Omega$;
  • $u^+$ and $u^-$ are the traces of the solution on the two sides of $\partial\Omega$;
  • $P^+(x,z)\coloneqq \frac{\partial G(x,z)}{\partial n^+_z}$ is the normal derivative (the Poisson kernel) with respect to the boundary point $z$;
  • $G(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the usual Green's function for the PDE;
  • and $\alpha(x)=\tfrac12$ for $x\in\partial\Omega$ while $\alpha(x)=1$ for interior points.

Reference: this formulation follows the standard double‑layer/single‑layer decomposition in boundary integral theories for open surfaces (Costabel, 1987). [Costabel 1987]

Why the naive single‑sided trick fails

The tempting shortcut is: "just treat the surface like a usual closed boundary and ignore the minus side." Bad idea. If the solution has a jump across the surface (and it generally will when you have physical transmission or open geometry), the single‑sided BIE loses the $[u^+-u^-]$ and $[\partial_n u^+ - \partial_n u^-]$ terms — those are exactly the things that encode the jump. Ignoring them biases the solution; it isn't a subtle effect, it kills correctness on open domains.

How we adapt WalkOnStars (the high‑level plan)

  1. Keep the Green's volume term estimation exactly as before: sample stars (spherical/ball escapes) in the interior and estimate the volumetric integral $\int_\Omega G(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y$ with unbiased Monte Carlo.
  2. For the boundary integrals, treat them as expectations over surface points: the two boundary integrals above are surface expectations of known kernels against the unknown boundary traces and their normal derivatives. So we interpret each boundary integral as a Monte Carlo average over sampled boundary locations.
  3. Unlike the closed‑boundary case, the unknowns are now the two traces $u^+$ and $u^-$ plus the two normal derivatives on the surface. Practically this means the integral equation couples both sides of the surface and must be solved for these traces (still without meshing the interior volume). WalkOnStars supplies unbiased estimators of the integral operators acting on these unknown traces.
  4. Implementation note (practical but boring): sampling the surface and evaluating the kernels $P^+(x,z)$ and $G(x,z)$ is straightforward if you have a nearest‑point and normal oracle for the surface. That keeps us mesh‑free. The hard part is solving the resulting integral equation for the unknown boundary data — we use iterative linear solvers that accept Monte Carlo estimates of the operator actions (more variance but no mesh).

What to expect — the fine print

  • Variance goes up. Estimating both single‑ and double‑layer contributions stochastically increases estimator variance compared to the closed/Dirichlet case. You can reduce variance with importance sampling on the surface or control variates, but there is no free lunch.
  • Corners and edges are nasty. The $\alpha=\tfrac12$ jump and the boundary kernels have singular behaviour at sharp features; special handling (singularity‑aware sampling or local refinement of the boundary estimator) is required to avoid blowup in variance or bias.
  • We kept it honest: this stays grid‑free and doesn't expand the solution in a fixed basis, but you trade that freedom for a larger system of unknown boundary traces to solve.

In short: walk on stars + double‑sided BIE = still mesh‑free and correct for open surfaces, but you pay in more unknowns and more variance. This is the price of admitting both sides of the wall.

Don't Trust the First Hit

The tempting shortcut is simple and cute: at a WoS step from $x_k$ take the Dirichlet radius $d_{\text{Dirichlet}}$, form the truncated ball $\ball(x_k,d_{\text{Dirichlet}})\cap\domain$, shoot a uniformly random ray out of $x_k$, and declare the next walk point to be the first intersection of that ray with the boundary of the truncated ball. It looks like a direct extension of the tricks in Simonov et al. (2008) and Ermakov et al. (2009), and at first glance it seems to avoid having to deal with the whole domain at once. [Simonov 2008; Ermakov 2009]

[Figure: fig:first_intersection_fail]

Why the idea is appealing

With a full sphere you get nice symmetry: the exit law of Brownian motion from a centered ball is simple (that symmetry is the whole point of classical WoS [Müller 1956]). So the naive move is to keep that local-symmetry mentality but clip the ball to the domain — sample the portion you can see, take the first hit, repeat. The hope is that this local procedure still mimics the correct exit distribution without building or meshing the whole boundary.

Why it breaks in nonconvex domains

Short version: visibility creates geometry-induced bias and spikes. The boundary of the truncated subdomain $\genset=\ball(x_k,d_{\text{Dirichlet}})\cap\domain$ is not a smooth, symmetric sphere anymore — it has two qualitatively different pieces:

  • the visible part of the spherical boundary (the spherical cap(s) you can actually see from $x_k$), and
  • the silhouette / visibility edges where obstacles in the domain cut into the sphere — these are the so-called crepuscular rays or visibility silhouettes studied in computational geometry. [Gargallo 2007]

When you take the "first intersection along a random ray," you are implicitly sampling with respect to the angular measure seen from $x_k$, not the harmonic measure that Brownian motion would produce on $\partial\genset$. Those two measures only coincide in very special symmetric situations (a full ball or convex domain with certain placements). In a nonconvex setting the angular sampling piles probability onto the visibility silhouette: rays that skim obstacles or just graze silhouette edges cause a concentration of hits and produce singular behavior (sharp peaks) in the exit-density on $\partial\genset$.

Practical consequences (what actually goes wrong)

  • Bias: The sampled exit distribution is not the Brownian exit law (harmonic measure) for $\genset$, so the estimator becomes biased. This isn't a small perturbation — geometry can produce O(1) deviations localized on silhouette curves.
  • High variance and instability: Probability mass concentrates near visibility edges, so Monte Carlo estimates get noisy and require many samples to quiet down.
  • Nonlocal dependence: The error at $x_k$ depends on occluders far away along the ray directions, so you can't fix it by only tweaking local sampling rules without accounting for global geometry.

Look, we tried to make this work on toy nonconvex domains and the behavior is obvious: the first-intersection trick systematically misallocates mass toward silhouettes (see figure), producing biased solutions. We're not claiming there's no clever correction, but the naive first-intersection rule simply breaks the core probabilistic identity that WoS relies on.

TL;DR

The naive first-intersection sampler feels like an easy extension of earlier WoS variants [Simonov 2008; Ermakov 2009], but visibility-induced crepuscular effects create singular, non-harmonic exit distributions in nonconvex domains [Gargallo 2007]. In short: cute idea, broken in practice — so we don't use it. Instead we revert to estimators that respect the correct exit law (see BranchingEstimator and the method description elsewhere in this paper).

Don't Reuse the Same Sample for Neumann — Here's Why

Short version: we draw two independent samples, x_{k+1} and z_{k+1}, because trying to estimate both the unknown solution term (the part multiplying u) and the known Neumann term (the part multiplying h) from a single geometric sample introduces a bias. Yes, two samples cost a bit more variance/more computation, but that's the price of staying honest — one-sample tricks look attractive but quietly change the target you're estimating.

The naive idea (and why it tempts you)

The naive move is: use the same WoSt direction/sample x_{k+1} to estimate both boundary integrals in the boundary integral estimator. That feels efficient — fewer random draws, less bookkeeping. In symbols you would rewrite the estimator to evaluate both terms at the same point x_{k+1}.

Why that breaks (the core intuition)

It breaks because the direction-sampling routine RandomWalkStarShapedDomains never actually proposes boundary points that lie on a flat Neumann patch when the current walker is sitting on that Neumann boundary (i.e., when x_k \in \partial\domain_N), even if the true Neumann data h is nonzero there. Concretely, our sampling density on the spherical boundary, call it p^{\ssdboundary}, is proportional to the Poisson kernel P^B or, intuitively, the solid angle subtended by a boundary patch. For boundary points where the normal is perpendicular to the ray from x_k — grazing or tangential points — that solid angle is zero, so the sampler gives them zero probability. So any contribution coming from h on those parts of the boundary is simply never seen by the estimator.

Formal way to see the bias

If you force the single-sample trick, you're implicitly replacing the true Neumann data h by a modified version that zeros out any Neumann values on boundary patches the sampler never hits. We denote that modified Neumann function by:

$$ \label{eq:NeumannTilde} \tilde{h}(x_{k+1}) \coloneqq \begin{cases} h(x_{k+1}), & x_{k+1} \in \ssdboundary_N(x_k,r), \\ 0, & x_{k+1} \in \ssdboundary_\ball(x_k,r). \end{cases} $$

In plain language: any Neumann contribution sitting on the spherical cap that our solid-angle sampler assigns zero mass to gets set to zero in the estimator. That's not variance — that's bias. You're no longer solving the original boundary-value problem.

So what do we do?

  • We use two independent samples, x_{k+1} to estimate the term tied to the unknown u, and an independent z_{k+1} to estimate the known Neumann term h. That keeps the estimator unbiased with respect to the true boundary data.
  • Yes, this increases per-step sampling cost and can increase variance compared to the optimistic one-sample scheme. But variance is fixable (more samples, control variates, etc.); bias is not — if you bias the estimator you solve a different PDE.

Honest caveat

We're not pretending this is the only possible fix. In principle you could redesign the sampling density to give positive mass to those tangential boundary points or apply unbiased importance-weight corrections. In practice those changes complicate the geometry-aware sampling (and may reintroduce other problems), so the simple, robust route is separate sampling for the Neumann term. Empirically this keeps the method faithful to the original mixed boundary-value problem.

Pseudocode (how to find the Neumann silhouette without losing your mind)

Short version: given a query point $x\in\mathbb{R}^3$ and a search radius $r^{max}$, we want the closest point on the visibility silhouette of the Neumann boundary $\partial\Omega_N$ that is visible from $x$. The naive approach—scan every boundary triangle and test visibility—is simple but painfully slow. Instead we recurse a spatial tree (called snch here), prune subtrees with cheap distance bounds, and only do exact visibility tests on promising elements.

Top-level API

Signature:

  • AlgoSilhouetteDistanceNeumann(x, r^{max}=\infty) -> (p^*, d^*)
  • Returns the closest visible silhouette point p^* (or null) and its distance d^* ≤ r^{max}.

High-level idea

The recursive routine DistClosestSilhouette(node, x, r^{max}) does three things:

  1. Quickly reject whole nodes whose bounding volume is farther than the current best distance.
  2. If a node is internal, visit children in order of increasing lower-bound distance (best-first pruning helps a lot in practice).
  3. If a node is a leaf, test each contained boundary element (edge/triangle silhouette candidate) for a visible closest point and update the best-found result.

Pseudocode (readable, not line-noise)

We present a compact, implementation-friendly form. Variable bestDist is initially r^{max} and holds the current closest distance; bestPoint is the corresponding point (or null).

  1. AlgoSilhouetteDistanceNeumann(x, r^{max}=\infty):
    1. Set bestDist := r^{max}, bestPoint := null.
    2. Call DistClosestSilhouette(snch.root, x, bestDist, bestPoint).
    3. Return (bestPoint, bestDist).
  2. DistClosestSilhouette(node, x, bestDist, bestPoint):
    1. Compute a cheap lower bound distance d_min from x to node's bounding volume (sphere or AABB). If d_min >= bestDist then prune: return.
    2. If node is internal:
      1. Compute lower bounds for children and sort children by that bound (ascending).
      2. For each child in that order, recurse: DistClosestSilhouette(child, x, bestDist, bestPoint). Update bestDist / bestPoint when children find better results.
    3. Else (leaf):
      1. For each boundary element $e$ stored in the leaf (candidate silhouette segment/triangle):
        1. Compute the closest point $p_e$ on the geometric feature $e$ to $x$ (this is a standard point-to-triangle / point-to-edge nearest-point computation).
        2. If $\|x-p_e\| \geq bestDist$, skip $e$ (cheap reject).
        3. Perform a visibility test: is the segment $[x,p_e]$ unobstructed, and does $p_e$ lie on the actual visibility silhouette of $\partial\Omega_N$? (See notes below on the test.)
        4. If visible and $\|x-p_e\| < bestDist$, set bestDist := \|x-p_e\| and bestPoint := p_e.

Visibility test (practical notes)

Two checks are required to decide if a candidate point p_e is a valid silhouette point visible from x:

  1. Line-of-sight: test whether the open segment $(x,p_e)$ intersects the domain interior or other boundary patches that would block visibility. This is a segment-mesh intersection test; use conservative early exits (bounding-volume checks) and robust predicates to avoid floating-point pathologies.
  2. Silhouette membership: the silhouette is where the local boundary normal becomes tangent to the view direction. In practice we check the sign (or dot) of the normal with the view direction on adjacent boundary elements or test whether clipping the element by the view half-space produces an edge at $p_e$. If geometry is degenerate (nearly tangent), apply a small geometric epsilon and treat the case carefully.

Implementation gotchas and honest limitations

  • Small, sharp features and nearly tangent views are the usual troublemakers. The algorithm is fast in smooth regions but needs special handling (epsilons, exact predicates, or local refinement) near corners or cusps.
  • Ordering children by their lower bound helps a lot because a good early bestDist prunes many nodes. If you don't do this, the tree still works but performance suffers.
  • We avoid scanning the whole mesh, but you still need a robust segment-mesh intersection routine. Floating point errors here produce incorrect visibility decisions; plan for that.
  • This procedure returns a closest silhouette point within the provided radius. If you want the absolute global nearest silhouette, call with r^{max}=\infty (or a very large bound), but expect more work in pathological meshes.

Why this design?

The naive brute-force check is simple but O(N) per query. The tree + pruning approach gives you logarithmic-ish behavior on typical geometry because most nodes are rejected by cheap bounds. We admit it's not perfect around degeneracies, and the visibility test is the real bottleneck — but empirically this pattern is what lets grid-free Monte Carlo remain practical on complex domains.
