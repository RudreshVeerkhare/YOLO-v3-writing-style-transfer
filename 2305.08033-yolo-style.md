  ## What Others Have Tried

  The idea that some wave equations play nicely with conformal (angle-preserving) transformations is old, boring, and useful. The first documented instance of conformal invariance for wave-like equations on Minkowski spacetime appears in the Maxwell equations, discovered by Cunningham and Bateman [Cunningham:1910:PRE, Bateman:1910:TEE]. The spacetime maps behind that invariance are what people call spherical wave transformations; geometers later studied them in the context of Lie sphere geometry [cecil2008lie].

  Those early observations didn’t stay isolated. Researchers expanded the story to other field equations [Dirac:1936:WEC, Mclennan:1956:CIC, Lomont:1961:CIM], and these extensions include the scalar wave equation we care about. The source text cuts off mid-sentence after "However, the"; we won’t pretend to know what the authors meant there. A plausible guess (pure speculation) is that they were about to mention limitations or specific generalizations, but that’s not stated explicitly in the original document.

  On the numerical side, people have been wrestling with infinite domains for decades. Over the past half-century a variety of numerical schemes have been proposed to handle waves on unbounded domains. The original text says it will "briefly discuss two conventional approaches" to the infinite-domain problem but doesn’t list them in the excerpt we were given. From the broader literature (and to set context for our method), standard practical strategies include truncating the domain and adding artificial boundary conditions, or transforming/compactifying the domain so infinity gets moved to a finite location. We won’t try to re-run the entire literature review here — for a thorough survey see [tsynkov1998numerical, seibel2022boundary].

  In short: the conformal symmetry story is old and well-documented for Maxwell and other wave equations; numerical tricks for infinite domains are many, and the details matter. We use the historical facts above as the springboard for our approach, which leans on a particular spacetime transformation to avoid truncation. More on that in the methods section.

## What's This About

We want the obvious but annoying thing: numerically solve the scalar wave equation on the entire, unbounded Minkowski spacetime without cheating by cutting the domain off or slapping on artificial boundaries. In plain terms: given compactly supported initial data and possibly some obstacles, how do you compute the solution everywhere in space and forward in time using only finite time and memory?

Here we apply a previously stated corollary (referenced as Corollary [cor1] in the original notes) to the initial boundary value problem (IBVP) for wave propagation on infinite domains. The corollary supplies the mathematical property — a conformal/Kelvin-type invariance in Minkowski spacetime — that lets us move between the infinite and a bounded problem. The rest is bookkeeping: transform the problem, solve on a finite grid, and map the solution back.

Note: the original text points to Corollary [cor1] but does not reproduce it here. We therefore use that result as a black box: it gives the conformal mapping and the exact transformation rules needed to carry the IBVP to a bounded domain.

## Unbounded problem (the whole-space IBVP)

Here is the concrete infinite-domain IBVP we want to solve. Let $B\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ be a bounded set (possibly empty) representing obstacles. The spatial domain is the exterior $$\mathbb{R}^n\setminus B.$$We are given compactly supported initial data$f,h$on the exterior and boundary data$p$on the obstacle boundary$\partial B$. The unknown is$$u(\mathbf x,t)\quad\text{for }(\mathbf x,t)\in(\mathbb{R}^n\setminus B)\times[t_0,\infty).$$

Formally, the IBVP is

$$\begin{cases}
\square_{(\vec x,t)}u(\vec x,t) = 0,&\vec x\in \mathbb{R}^n\setminus B,\ t\ge t_0,\\
(\hat L u)(\vec x,t) = p(\vec x),&\vec x\in\partial B,\ t>t_0,\\
u
u u(\vec x,t_0) = f(\vec x),&\vec x\in\mathbb{R}^n\setminus B,\\
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}(\vec x,t_0) = h(\vec x),&\vec x\in\mathbb{R}^n\setminus B,
\end{cases}$$

where$\square_{(\vec x,t)}$is the usual wave operator in Minkowski spacetime, and$\hat L$ is a fixed linear boundary operator encoding the obstacle boundary condition (e.g. Dirichlet, Neumann, or something more exotic). Nothing fancy here — it's the standard exterior wave IBVP, stated for completeness.

## How We Squash Infinity (Inversion)

Big idea, one sentence: exploit a Minkowski–Kelvin/Möbius inversion that is a conformal symmetry of the scalar wave equation, use it to map the infinite exterior domain into a bounded domain, solve there, then map back.

Concretely, let $\Phi$ be the Möbius transformation (denoted $\Inv$ in the source) — the Minkowski inversion map — with conformal factor $\varphi_{\Inv}$ (also given in the original material). Apply this map to the unbounded domain $\Omega$ to obtain the inverted, bounded domain $\Omega' = \Inv(\Omega)$. The inversion moves the obstacle and the initial surface to corresponding sets inside a finite region. For a two-dimensional spatial example see

  .

Under this inversion the wave equation preserves its zero-right-hand-side form up to the conformal factor. After the change of variables and the associated rescaling of the field, the transformed unknown $V(\vec\xi,\tau)$ satisfies the free wave equation on the bounded domain:

$$\square_{(\vec \xi,\tau)} V(\vec\xi,\tau) = 0,\qquad(\vec\xi,\tau)\in\Omega'.$$

Boundary and initial conditions map according to the theorem used (referred to as thm:MinkowskiKelvin in the original). For example, an obstacle boundary and its condition are pulled back to some$S'_{\rm obs}\subset\partial\Omega'$and an associated transformed condition. The original references to the precise formulas are present in the paper but not reproduced here; we treat the transformation rules as given by that theorem.

Short caveat: the original text references equations for$\Inv$and$\varphi_{\Inv}$ but does not restate them here; you'll need those explicit formulas to implement the mapping numerically. We don't invent them for you.

        
      

    

      

        
        
## How We Actually Solve It

High-level recipe:

Use the inversion map $\Inv$ to get the bounded domain $\Omega'$. Transform the initial and boundary data accordingly.
- Discretize $\Omega'$ (choose a grid or point cloud).
- Initialize $V$ and $\partial_\tau V$ near the transformed initial surface $S'_{\rm init}$ from the mapped initial data.
- Integrate the wave equation forward on $\Omega'$ with a standard time-stepping solver (e.g. FDTD or any suitable finite-difference/time-integration method).
- To query the solution on the original, unbounded domain: map your query point $(\mathbf x,t)$ to its preimage $(\vec\xi,\tau)=\Inv(\mathbf x,t)$, evaluate $V(\vec\xi,\tau)$ by interpolation on the numerical grid, and recover $u(\mathbf x,t)$ by applying the analytic scaling factor $G(\vec\xi,\tau)$ produced by the conformal transform.

That last step is summarized symbolically as

$$u(\mathbf x,t) = G\big(\Inv(\mathbf x,t)\big)\,V\big(\Inv(\mathbf x,t)\big).$$

Here$G(\cdot)$is given analytically by the transformation theorem; it encodes the conformal factor and any field rescaling.$V(\cdot)$is obtained numerically and typically requires spatial interpolation to evaluate at arbitrary preimage points.

On accuracy: using a second-order accurate spatial and temporal discretization (e.g. centered finite differences in space and time), the leading-order numerical error scales like

$$\text{error} = \mathcal{O}(\Delta\xi^2) + \mathcal{O}(\Delta\tau^2),$$

where$\Delta\xi$and$\Delta\tau$are the spatial and temporal grid spacings on the bounded domain$\Omega'$. This is the same behavior you'd expect from a textbook second-order FDTD scheme; the inversion doesn't magically remove discretization error.

Practical notes and missing bits:

- Finding the preimage$(\vec\xi,\tau)=\Inv(\mathbf x,t)$for arbitrary$(\mathbf x,t)$is straightforward algebraically from the inversion formulas, but the original text omits the implementation details—especially corner cases where the mapping approaches the conformal singularity. Handle those carefully.
- The original write-up points to an appendix (app:cost_mk) for cost estimates and to an algorithm box (Algorithm [alg:KelvinWave2]) summarizing the solver. Those items are not reproduced here; consult them for complexity and practical scheduling decisions.
- Interpolation and boundary handling on$\Omega'$ are standard engineering work; beware that irregular boundaries inherited from the inversion may require unstructured-grid techniques or careful padding.

  ## Wrapping Up

  We presented a mathematical way to compute wave propagation on an unbounded spacetime domain using only finite time and memory. In plain English: instead of pretending the universe ends at the edge of our grid, we use a transformation so the whole infinite domain behaves like a finite one for numerical work. It’s neat, it works under certain assumptions, and it saves you from designing clever artificial boundaries. No smoke and mirrors — just a coordinate trick applied carefully.

  Important assumptions (don’t skip these): our derivation and numerical recipe assume a homogeneous, motionless medium with no viscothermal dissipation, and the dynamics are governed by the scalar wave equation (referenced in the text as eq:wavePDE). In other words, linear scalar waves in an idealized medium. If your problem has strong damping, moving media, anisotropy, or nonlinear coupling, this method as stated is not guaranteed to apply.

  Limitations, stated honestly: this is a mathematical construction tailored to the scalar wave equation in the idealized setting above. It is not a drop-in cure for every wave problem. You get exact handling of the infinite domain only because the PDE and the transformation play nicely together under our assumptions. Break that friendship (add dissipation, inhomogeneity, nonlinear terms, or other physics) and all bets are off.

  The manuscript ends with an unfinished sentence about whether similar transformations exist for other types of wave equations: "It is an open question whether there exist transformations of other types of wave equations which preserve the equation of motion but not the domain boundedn" — the original text cuts off there. We will not invent the missing words. A plausible reading is that the authors are asking whether there are analogous mappings that leave the form of other wave equations invariant while mapping infinite domains to finite ones; that is a reasonable research direction, but we label that as speculation because the paper does not finish the thought or provide further details.

  So what’s the takeaway? This is basically a slightly fancier version of "map the infinite to the finite and solve it there" that actually preserves the scalar wave equation under the stated assumptions. It’s not huge, but it’s useful if you care about exact infinite-domain behavior for idealized scalar waves. Future work — in the obvious ways — is to see how far this approach can be pushed: other PDEs, dissipative physics, moving or inhomogeneous backgrounds, and nonlinearities. We don’t have answers yet; we just point out the sensible next steps.

  ## Why the Minkowski Inversion is Conformal

  High level: we need to show the inversion map $\Phi=\Inv$ rescales the Minkowski metric by a scalar at every point. In other words, the pullback metric $\Phi^*\eta$ equals $\varphi_{\Inv}^2\,\eta$, where the scalar factor $\varphi_{\Inv}$ is the one given in (eq:Inv_factor). This is routine linear-algebra-with-a-twist: compute the Jacobian, shove it into the formula for the pullback, and watch the algebra collapse to a scalar multiple of the original metric.

  ### Step-by-step

  Take two arbitrary tangent vectors $\vec u,\vec v$ in Minkowski spacetime $\mathbb R^{n,1}$ equipped with the metric matrix $\mathbb I_{n,1}=\operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1,-1)$ (we also use $\mathbb I_{n+1}=\operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1)$ for the identity). By definition of the pullback,

  $$(\Phi^*\eta)(\vec u,\vec v) 
  = \eta\big(d\Phi\,\vec u,\;d\Phi\,\vec v\big)
  = \vec u^{\!T}\,d\Phi^{\!T}\,\mathbb I_{n,1}\,d\Phi\,\vec v.$$

  The Jacobian of the inversion has the convenient closed form (this is the algebraic heart of the map):

  $$d\Phi 
  = \varphi_{\Inv}\Big(\mathbb I_{n+1} - 2\,\varphi_{\Inv}\,\vec v\,\vec v^{\!T}\,\mathbb I_{n,1}\Big).$$

  Plugging this into the pullback formula gives

  $$(\Phi^*\eta)(\vec u,\vec v)
  = \varphi_{\Inv}^2\;\vec u^{\!T}\Big(\mathbb I_{n+1} - 2\varphi_{\Inv}\,\vec v\,\vec v^{\!T}\,\mathbb I_{n,1}\Big)^{\!T}\mathbb I_{n,1}\Big(\mathbb I_{n+1} - 2\varphi_{\Inv}\,\vec v\,\vec v^{\!T}\,\mathbb I_{n,1}\Big)\vec v.$$

  Now the slightly tedious but straightforward algebra — using the exact definition of$\varphi_{\Inv}$given in (eq:Inv_factor) — reduces the middle matrix to$\mathbb I_{n,1}$. In plain English: after expansion the rank-one correction terms cancel exactly because$\varphi_{\Inv}$was chosen to make them do so. Therefore the whole expression becomes

  $$(\Phi^*\eta)(\vec u,\vec v) = \varphi_{\Inv}^2\;\vec u^{\!T}\,\mathbb I_{n,1}\,\vec v = \varphi_{\Inv}^2\,\eta(\vec u,\vec v).$$

  That holds for arbitrary tangent vectors$\vec u,\vec v$, so

  $$\Phi^*\eta = \varphi_{\Inv}^2\,\eta,$$

  and$\Phi$is conformal with conformal factor$\varphi_{\Inv}$(as claimed). 

  We won't belabor the matrix expansion here — it's algebra, not insight. If you want the dirty details, expand the product using the displayed expression for$d\Phi$ and check the cancellations; they hinge directly on the formula in (eq:Inv_factor).

        
      

    

      

        
        

  ## Minkowski, But With Infinity Sewn On

  We run into a predictable nuisance: the Kelvin (inversion) map that helps us move infinity to a finite place is perfectly well-behaved everywhere except on the light cone. The light cone
  is the set
  $$C_0 = \{(x,t) \in \RR^{n,1} \mid |x|^2 - t^2 = 0\},$$and inversion is not defined there in the usual picture. So we fix that by gluing a copy of the light cone at infinity and declaring that inversion swaps the two.

  Formally, we build a one-cone extension of Minkowski spacetime by taking a disjoint copy of that cone, call it$C_\infty$, and adjoining it to the original spacetime. In symbols:

  $$\overline{\RR^{n,1}} \coloneqq \RR^{n,1} \sqcup C_\infty.$$

  Think of$C_\infty$as a second sheet that mirrors the original light cone$C_0$. We introduce an identification map$$j:\;C_0 \to C_\infty$$and define the restriction of the inversion map on$C_0 \cup C_\infty$simply by swapping points across$j$(points on$C_0$go to their partners in$C_\infty$and vice versa). This is the minimal surgery that makes inversion a total map.

  Why do this? Intuitively: inversion tries to send some directions to infinity and others to the origin; the light cone is the boundary between time- and space-like directions. By gluing a cone at infinity and making inversion flip the two cones, the inversion becomes continuous (and well-defined) on the extended space.

  We then choose the topology on$\overline{\RR^{n,1}}$so that the inversion map is continuous. With that topology in place, the usual spacetime symmetries—Lorentz transformations, translations, and scalings—extend continuously to$\overline{\RR^{n,1}}$as well, so nothing breaks when we move to the compactified picture.

  ### Concrete recipe (how to think about implementing this)

  

    - Start with the standard Minkowski space$\RR^{n,1}$and its light cone$C_0$.

    - Make a disjoint copy$C_\infty$and an identification map$j:C_0\to C_\infty$.

    - Define inversion on$\RR^{n,1}\setminus C_0$as usual; on$C_0\cup C_\infty$define it by swapping via$j$.

    - Equip$\overline{\RR^{n,1}}=\RR^{n,1}\sqcup C_\infty$with a topology that agrees with the standard one on$\RR^{n,1}$and makes inversion continuous at the glued cone.

    - Check that Lorentz transformations, translations and dilations extend continuously (they will, because they preserve the cone structure).

  

  ### The bits the paper skips (our honest confession)

  The original write-up gives the idea but does not spell out the neighborhood basis or the exact topology around points of$C_\infty$. We do not invent those missing technicalities here. One plausible and common choice is to declare neighborhoods of a point on$C_\infty$to correspond to complements of compact sets that approach the corresponding direction in$\RR^{n,1}$, but that is an interpretation — not a stated fact in the source. If you need full rigor, you'll want an explicit chart construction or a reference to a standard compactification construction.

  So: this is basically a slightly fancier version of "attach the problematic boundary so maps become total." It's ugly, but it works, and it makes the inversion and the symmetry group play nicely together on the extended spacetime.

        
      

    

      

        
        

  ## Why the Wave Equation Doesn't Care About the Kelvin Map

  High level: we only need to check one awkward map and everything else is automatic. The scalar wave equation is already invariant under Lorentz transforms, translations, and scaling — boring but true. So to prove invariance under the full Minkowski–Kelvin family it suffices to handle the inversion map \(\Phi(\vec\xi,\tau)=\Inv(\vec\xi,\tau)\) (see eq:Inv) and its conformal factor \(\varphi_{\Inv}\) (see eq:Inv_factor).

  We prove the claim in exterior-calculus language because it is compact and unambiguous. The wave equation can be written as

  $$d\star_1 dU = 0,$$

  where \(\star_1\) is the Hodge star for the standard Minkowski metric on \(\mathbb{R}^{n,1}_{(x,t)}\). After applying the inversion map we are working with the pulled-back ("inverted") spacetime and its Hodge star, which we denote \(\star_1'\).

  Now apply the pullback \(\Inv^*\) to the wave equation. Pullbacks commute with the exterior derivative, so the pulled-back equation reads

  $$d\,\Inv^*(\star_1'\,dU)=0.$$

  For brevity the original manuscript writes this as

  $$d\Tilde{\star}_1' dU = 0,\qquad$$

  where \(\Tilde{\star}_1'\) denotes the Hodge star of the inverted spacetime after being pulled back to the original coordinates.

  What's left is bookkeeping: the pulled-back Hodge star \(\Inv^*(\star_1')\) differs from the original \(\star_1\) by a (computable) power of the conformal factor \(\varphi_{\Inv}\). In other words, there is an identity of the form

  \(\Inv^*(\star_1') = \varphi_{\Inv}^{\;\kappa(n)}\,\star_1\),

  where the exponent \(\kappa(n)\) is a known function of the spacetime dimension \(n\). Plugging this relation into the pulled-back equation converts \(d\Tilde{\star}_1' dU=0\) into an equation involving \(\star_1\) and powers of \(\varphi_{\Inv}\). With the standard algebra one then checks that the appropriate rescaling of the field (by the usual conformal power depending on \(n\)) turns the pulled-back equation into the original wave equation again. So the inversion is conformal for the scalar wave operator, and together with the trivial invariances (Lorentz, translations, scaling) this proves theorem thm:MinkowskiKelvin.

  Two honest caveats:

  

    - The original short proof omits the explicit exponent \(\kappa(n)\) and the exact rescaling factor for \(U\); those are routine but mechanical to write down and depend on dimension. We do not invent them here — they are computable from the standard formula for how the Hodge star transforms under a conformal change of metric.

    - If you need the line-by-line algebra (pullback of the Hodge star, the exact power of \(\varphi_{\Inv}\), and the rescaling of \(U\)), we can expand this into the explicit formulas. It is not philosophically deep; it's just index gymnastics.

  

  So: inversion checked (mod the routine Hodge-star algebra), other symmetries are trivial, theorem proven. Not glamorous, but it is what it is.

        
      

    

      

        
        

  ## Counting Grid Points (The Boring Cost)

  High-level: we take the unbounded Minkowski domain \(\Omega\), push it through the Kelvin inversion so that \(\Omega' = \Inv(\Omega)\) is bounded, and then solve the initial-boundary value problem on \(\Omega'\) with a straightforward FDTD scheme. That gives us a finite computational problem — yay — but it still costs time and memory. Here we quantify that cost.

  Let \(\Delta\xi\) be the spatial sampling interval on the rectilinear grid in the bounded coordinates and \(\Delta\tau\) the time step. If we need to sample the entire bounded domain \(\Omega'\) then the number of spatial grid points \(M\) and the number of time steps \(N\) are (in the same form as the original text):

  $$M \approx \left\lceil \frac{\xi_0 + \tau_0}{\Delta \xi} \right\rceil^n ,\\
  N \approx \left\lceil \frac{\tau_0}{\Delta \tau} \right\rceil ,$$

  Informally: \(M\) is the number of spatial samples per time slice (raised to the spatial dimension \(n\)), and \(N\) is how many time slices we march through. The strange-looking \(\xi_0 + \tau_0\) numerator is just the effective spatial extent that must be covered by the grid: you need to cover the original spatial extent \(\xi_0\) plus the distance signals can travel during the simulated time \(\tau_0\) (thinking in units where wave speed is 1 makes this addition natural). This is an intuition, not a new theorem — it explains why the spatial and temporal extents appear together.

  Important caveat: the original excerpt references "subequations" that define \(\xi_0\) and \(\tau_0\) but does not include those definitions here. One plausible interpretation is that \(\xi_0\) and \(\tau_0\) are the spatial and temporal extents of the bounded domain \(\Omega'\), but that is our interpretation, not a quoted fact from the missing text.

  What this buys you (and what it doesn't): the wall-clock work of an explicit FDTD solver scales like the number of grid updates, i.e. roughly \(\text{Work} = O(M\cdot N)\). Memory depends on whether you stream time (store only a few slices) or keep the whole spacetime lattice: streaming costs \(O(M)\), storing everything costs \(O(M\cdot N)\). The per-step constant hides the stencil size (how many neighbors you read/write) and implementation details — nothing magic here.

  One more practical note: for explicit FDTD schemes you also have a CFL condition tying \(\Delta\tau\) to \(\Delta\xi\). So if \(\Delta\tau\) must scale like \(\Delta\xi\) for stability, then \(N\) grows like \(1/\Delta\xi\) for fixed \(\tau_0\), and the total work scales like \(\Delta\xi^{-(n+1)}\). In plain English: halving the grid spacing in space typically multiplies the computational cost by about \(2^{n+1}\). That's not a bug, that's numerical PDEs.

  Bottom line: mapping the infinite domain to a bounded one makes the problem finite, but it does not make it cheap. You still pay polynomially in the inverse resolution, and the cost blows up as you refine. We didn't find any secret that avoids this — just a different coordinate system and the same numerical scaling, more or less.

Academic context and alternative approaches (critiques, complements, or contrasts)

([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.12942/lrr-2004-1?utm_source=openai))

          ### 🔧 Practical Applications

          Below is what I could find on practical code, demos, benchmarks, and deployments relevant to “Wave Simulations in Infinite Spacetime” (McKell, Nabizadeh, Wang, Chern, arXiv:2305.08033) and closely related techniques that solve wave equations on unbounded (infinite) domains by compactifying spacetime.

Primary paper and author resources
- Paper: Wave Simulations in Infinite Spacetime (arXiv:2305.08033). Describes the Möbius/Kelvin transform in Minkowski spacetime, a bounded-domain solve mapped back to the full infinite spacetime, and a finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) implementation outline with Algorithm 1. No official code link is given. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08033))
- Author pages mentioning the project and pointing to the arXiv:
  - Mohammad Sina Nabizadeh (MIT CSAIL) publication list. ([sinabiz.github.io](https://sinabiz.github.io/))
  - Chad McKell’s page lists the work as “In progress” with an arXiv link. ([chadmckell.com](https://chadmckell.com/?utm_source=openai))

Direct implementations of the same idea (closest matches)
- Kelvin Transformations for Simulations on Infinite Domains (SIGGRAPH 2021, Nabizadeh, Ramamoorthi, Chern). Project page with paper and supplementary material for solving Poisson/Laplace/Helmholtz on infinite domains via the Kelvin transform; this is the most direct code-adjacent predecessor to the 2023 “Infinite Spacetime” wave paper. The page hosts the paper and a supplementary ZIP (download from the site). ([cseweb.ucsd.edu](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~viscomp/projects/SIG21KelvinTransform/))
- On the Kelvin Transformation in Finite Difference Implementations (2020). Practical finite-difference discretization with a Kelvin mapping to virtually extend the domain to infinity; includes performance comparisons vs conventional truncated-domain approaches. Useful when adapting the 2023 idea to FD codes. ([mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/9/3/442?utm_source=openai))

Tutorials and runnable guides (infinite-domain wave equations)
- “How to solve wave equations on unbounded domains” (Dec 2023) by Anıl Zenginoğlu. Hands-on tutorial using hyperboloidal compactification (space and time transform) to put −∞…+∞ into a finite domain without artificial boundaries. Includes a Colab notebook linked from the page. Although not the exact Kelvin/Möbius transform of the 2023 paper, it targets the same goal: resolve full infinite-domain wave propagation and read off radiation at infinity. ([anilzen.github.io](https://anilzen.github.io/post/2023/waves-on-unbounded-domains/))

Open-source wave simulators that natively target very large/unbounded domains (relevant, widely used)
- wavesim (Modified Born Series for Helmholtz/Maxwell). CPU/GPU code; solves waves in arbitrarily large media and discusses CUDA acceleration and domain decomposition. While not the Minkowski–Kelvin method, it’s a practical, scalable infinite-extent frequency-domain solver used in optics. ([github.com](https://github.com/IvoVellekoop/wavesim?utm_source=openai))
- k-Wave (MATLAB toolbox). Time-domain acoustics (1D–3D); not a compactifying transform, but industrial-strength reference for wave solvers and boundary treatments. Useful as a baseline to compare against compactification methods. ([github.com](https://github.com/ucl-bug/k-wave?utm_source=openai))
- j-Wave (JAX-based differentiable acoustics). Open-source differentiable wave simulator; helpful for prototyping and benchmarking alternative formulations (FD/Fourier) on large domains. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.01499?utm_source=openai))
- GRDzhadzha (built on GRChombo). Evolves relativistic matter on fixed analytic spacetime backgrounds; related to wave propagation on curved spacetimes, though not the Möbius/Kelvin mapping itself. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08299?utm_source=openai))
- gwsim (MNRAS 2021). Finite element code for gravitational waves in a potential well; not a Kelvin/Möbius transform, but a practical computational wave solver for astrophysics that targets effectively unbounded settings. ([academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article-abstract/506/4/5278/6326637?utm_source=openai))
- NirvaWave (near-field wireless channel simulator) with code link in the paper. Practical wave propagation engine targeting large scenes and complex media; useful for contrasting transform-based approaches with fast scalar diffraction/Fourier methods. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11293?utm_source=openai))

PINN-based implementations for semi-infinite domains (adjacent to the goal)
- SeismicNet: Physics-informed neural networks for seismic wave modeling in semi-infinite domains; introduces absorbing boundary conditions as soft regularizers, temporal domain decomposition, and parametric loading surrogates. Includes code. ([github.com](https://github.com/paulpuren/seismic_modeling?utm_source=openai))
- PINNs_wave_equation (1D wave equation, forward/inverse problems). Good for experimentation with meshless solvers and custom coordinate transforms (including Kelvin-style factors) if you want to prototype the 2023 idea in a PINN. ([github.com](https://github.com/DCN-FAU-AvH/PINNs_wave_equation?utm_source=openai))
